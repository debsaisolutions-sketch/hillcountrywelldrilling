Buying Land? The One Water Well Lesson Every Landowner Wishes They Knew Sooner

Every week, our drilling crews meet with landowners who say some version of the same thing: "I wish someone had told me this before I signed the paperwork." Buying raw land is exciting, but when it comes to water access, a little bit of due diligence upfront can save you thousands of dollars and months of frustration later. Here's the biggest lesson we've learned from years of drilling wells across the region — and what you should know before you buy.

The Land Might Be Beautiful, But What's Underneath It?

The most common regret we hear is this: buyers fall in love with a piece of land based on the view, the trees, or the price — without ever asking whether it can actually produce a reliable water supply. Not all land sits above accessible groundwater. Some properties require wells drilled 400+ feet deep to hit water, while a neighboring parcel might strike a strong aquifer at 100 feet. That difference can mean a cost swing of many thousands of dollars.

Rock formations, clay layers, and local geology all play a role in how easy — or difficult — it is to find water. Unfortunately, this information usually isn't listed on a real estate flyer.

What We Recommend Before You Sign Anything

If you're considering buying vacant land, especially in rural or semi-rural areas without municipal water service, here's what can save you from an expensive surprise:

  • Talk to a local well driller before closing. We can often tell you what depths and yields are typical in a specific area based on wells we've drilled nearby.
  • Ask neighbors about their wells. How deep are they? What's their water quality like? Have they ever run dry during drought years?
  • Check public well records. Many states and counties keep logs of well depths and yields for permitted wells. This data is often free and public.
  • Consider a test well or hydrogeological survey. For larger or higher-stakes purchases, this can confirm water availability before you commit.
  • Understand water rights in your area. Some states have restrictions on how much groundwater you can pump, especially for agricultural or irrigation use.

Septic Setbacks and Well Placement

Another surprise that catches buyers off guard: well placement isn't just about finding water — it's also about local setback requirements from septic systems, property lines, and other structures. We've seen buyers purchase land only to discover that the only viable well location is uncomfortably close to where they planned to build their house, or that a future septic system will conflict with the ideal well site.

Before finalizing your building plans, it's worth having a professional walk the property with you to identify the best well location relative to your septic design, driveway, and home footprint.

Water Quality Isn't Guaranteed Either

Depth and yield are only half the story. Even if a well produces plenty of water, that water may need treatment for iron, sulfur, hardness, or bacteria. Some areas are known for naturally occurring minerals that affect taste, staining, or plumbing longevity. Knowing this ahead of time helps you budget not just for drilling, but for any filtration or treatment system you might need down the road.

Budgeting for the Unknown

One of the hardest parts of buying undeveloped land is that you won't know your exact well cost until the drill actually goes into the ground. That said, a knowledgeable local driller can give you a realistic range based on wells drilled nearby, helping you avoid the shock of unexpected costs after closing. We always recommend building a buffer into your land budget for well drilling, casing, pump equipment, and water testing — even if everything goes smoothly.

The Bottom Line

The biggest "I wish I knew" moment for most landowners isn't really about drilling — it's about timing. The best time to learn about your land's water potential is before you buy, not after. A short conversation with a local well professional can uncover red flags, set realistic expectations, and help you negotiate with confidence.
